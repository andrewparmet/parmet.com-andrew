Vocal Harmony in Wicked

July 2, 2020 · Andrew Parmet

I was listening to the Wicked soundtrack today and was reminded of something I noticed a while ago. I haven't seen it mentioned anywhere for confirmation, but I did find this nice analysis by Robert Vieira in my search.

What I noticed was a simple and surprising reversal of vocal roles. In For Good, as the plot of the musical has wound down, Elphaba and Glinda enter a duet (3:45). After a brief unison Elphaba's mezzo-soprano rises above Glinda's soprano. This inversion seemed odd to me; I thought the natural stacking of the voices would be the opposite. But it got me thinking: as a story about moral ambiguity, could it make sense to elevate the Wicked Witch's lines over those of Glinda the Good? The passage ends with a return to unison, and I hunted through the soundtrack for another example of the two leads singing together.

I didn't have to search far: they sing together in Defying Gravity, another critical point in the plot. Elphaba and Glinda are briefly in unison (3:26), split (3:33), rejoin on "defying," and then split again (3:38). In the first split Elphaba takes the upper voice; in the second it's Glinda. A moment of hesitation follows in the recitative (3:55). Vieira notes this is "the last moment of hope and the first moment of bitter resentment" between Elphaba and Glinda. This is their decision point. From here forward Glinda is the Good Witch and Elphaba is the Wicked Witch. Perhaps accordingly, and again between unison sections, Glinda takes the upper voice and Elphaba the lower.

In a lovely way the part writing mirrors the moral arc of the musical: while first presented as unnecessarily extreme and perhaps leaning towards evil, Elphaba's character is revealed to be well-intentioned (if tragically executed) and thus her vocals eventually rise above the shallow tones of Glinda the Good. The reversal the musical promises is complete: the "untold true story of the witches of Oz" has turned the tables, complete with harmony.
